# Screen 2 — Testing Is Part of the Design

A constraint is not a finished product.

It is a design attempt.

You choose a problem.

You choose an attention target.

You change one meaningful part of the activity.

Then you watch.

Did the activity make the problem clearer?

Did players notice different information?

Did the behavior change?

Did the activity stay connected to the sport?

Did the constraint create useful learning or weird behavior?

This is not failure.

This is the work.

## Key Idea

Good practice design is not “set it and forget it.”

Good practice design is noticing, adapting, and committing to the next version.

# Screen 4 — What Should You Watch?

When you run the upgraded activity, do not watch everything.

Watch the thing your constraint was designed to change.

Return to your attention target.

Ask:

- Are players noticing it earlier?
- Are players noticing it more clearly?
- Are players acting on it more often?
- Are players communicating about it?
- Are players finding better solutions?
- Is the activity rewarding the intended behavior?
- Is the activity still connected to the sport?

## Key Idea

Watch the attention target first.

That tells you whether the constraint is doing its job.

# Screen 6 — The Four Common Outcomes

When you test a constraint, you usually see one of four outcomes.

## 1. It Is Too Easy

Players solve it quickly and the problem disappears.

## 2. It Is Too Hard

Players are overwhelmed and cannot engage with the problem.

## 3. It Gets Weird

Players follow the rule, but the activity no longer feels connected to the sport.

## 4. Nothing Changes

The constraint does not seem to shape attention, behavior, or decisions.

Each outcome gives you a different adjustment path.

## Key Idea

Do not abandon the idea too quickly.

First, name what happened.

---

# Screen 7 — Outcome 1: Too Easy

The activity may be too easy if:

- Players solve the problem immediately
- The constraint stops creating challenge
- The opponent is not strong enough
- The scoring becomes automatic
- The activity loses pressure
- Players no longer need to search or adapt

This is not bad.

It means you can increase the challenge.

## Possible Adjustments

- Add pressure
- Expand the space
- Reduce the bonus
- Add a stronger opponent challenge
- Move closer to the full game
- Require the behavior in a more difficult situation
- Increase the decision load

## Example

If players quickly find open support space in a low-pressure game, add opponent pressure or reduce the time they have to act.

## Key Idea

If it is too easy, make the problem more game-like or more demanding.

---

# Screen 8 — Outcome 2: Too Hard

The activity may be too hard if:

- Players look overwhelmed
- The problem happens too fast
- Players cannot identify what matters
- The success condition rarely happens
- The constraint creates confusion
- The opponent challenge is too strong
- Players stop searching and start guessing

This does not mean the constraint is useless.

It may mean the first version asked for too much too soon.

## Possible Adjustments

- Reduce pressure
- Shrink the space
- Slow the starting situation
- Use fewer players
- Make the success condition clearer
- Simplify the rule
- Use a more predictable opponent problem first

## Example

If players cannot find support options under heavy pressure, reduce the opponent advantage or start with more time to scan.

## Key Idea

If it is too hard, simplify the problem without removing the learning goal.

---

# Screen 9 — Outcome 3: It Gets Weird

The activity may be getting weird if:

- Players are gaming the scoring
- The activity no longer looks or feels like the sport
- Players chase the bonus instead of solving the game problem
- The rule creates unnatural behavior
- The coach needs a long explanation every round
- Players remember the rule but forget the game

This is common.

It usually means the constraint became louder than the sport problem.

## Possible Adjustments

- Remove one rule
- Simplify the scoring
- Make the success condition more game-connected
- Return to a more normal version of the activity
- Ask what the constraint was actually rewarding
- Keep the same attention target but choose a cleaner lever

## Example

If players yell random information just to earn a communication bonus, change the success condition to “early, useful information that clearly helps a teammate.”

## Key Idea

If it gets weird, reconnect the constraint to the sport problem.

---

# Screen 10 — Outcome 4: Nothing Changes

Nothing may change if:

- The constraint does not match the problem
- The scoring does not matter enough
- The attention target is unclear
- Players do not understand what counts
- The activity does not create the problem often enough
- The constraint rewards something unrelated
- The coach is watching the wrong signal

This is not a disaster.

It is feedback.

## Possible Adjustments

- Revisit the problem statement
- Clarify the attention target
- Strengthen the scoring
- Change the lever
- Change the starting situation
- Make the success condition more visible
- Ask players what they noticed

## Example

If a bonus point for support does not change anything, the bonus may not matter, or players may not know what useful support looks like.

## Key Idea

If nothing changes, the constraint probably needs a clearer connection to the problem.

# Screen 16 — The Adjustment Menu

Use this menu when you need a next version.

## To Increase Challenge

- Add pressure
- Add opponent complexity
- Increase space
- Reduce time
- Reduce the bonus
- Require the behavior in a harder situation
- Move toward the full game

## To Reduce Challenge

- Reduce pressure
- Make the space smaller or simpler
- Slow the starting situation
- Use fewer players
- Make the opponent more predictable
- Clarify what counts
- Remove extra rules

## To Reconnect to the Sport

- Remove artificial rules
- Use more normal scoring
- Add a real opponent problem
- Make the success condition game-relevant
- Ask whether the activity still teaches the target problem

## To Create a Clearer Signal

- Watch one behavior
- Ask one coaching question
- Clarify the attention target
- Strengthen the reward
- Change the lever

# Screen 18 — The Coaching Question During the Activity

Your coaching question from Module 5 can help players reflect.

But timing matters.

Do not stop every single moment.

Let players experience the problem.

Then use the question to point attention back to what matters.

## Useful Moments to Ask

- After a short round
- During a natural break
- After the target behavior appears
- After the same problem repeats
- Before restarting the next version
- When players are ready to reflect

## Less Useful Moments

- Every time someone makes a mistake
- Before players have experienced the problem
- When the question becomes a lecture
- When you already know you are not going to listen to their answer

## Key Idea

Ask questions to guide attention, not to perform coaching intelligence.

# Screen 20 — What to Ask Players After the Activity

After the activity, ask questions that help players connect the constraint to the game problem.

Good questions include:

- What did the constraint make you notice?
- What changed in the activity?
- What became easier?
- What became harder?
- What did the opponent give you?
- What information helped?
- What option did you miss at first?
- What would you try next round?
- How did the scoring change your choices?
- When did the problem show up most clearly?

## Key Idea

Player answers are data.

They show you what the constraint actually made available to them.

# Screen 25 — Do Not Overcorrect Too Quickly

A common mistake is changing the constraint too soon.

Players need time to adapt.

If you change the rules every two minutes, they may never settle into the problem.

Before changing the constraint, ask:

- Did players understand the task?
- Did they get enough repetitions?
- Did the problem show up clearly?
- Did I watch the attention target?
- Did I give the constraint time to work?
- Am I adjusting because of evidence or impatience?

## Key Idea

Do not change everything because the first few reps were messy.

Messy is often part of learning.

---

# Screen 26 — When to Keep the Constraint

Keep the constraint for another round if:

- Players are beginning to notice the target information
- The problem is showing up clearly
- The activity still feels connected to the sport
- Players are searching for better solutions
- The challenge is appropriate
- The behavior is changing slowly but meaningfully

## Example

Players are starting to look for support space, but they are not using it consistently yet.

That may be a reason to keep going, not change the task.

## Key Idea

If the constraint is creating the right search, give it time.

---

# Screen 27 — When to Change the Constraint

Change the constraint if:

- Players are completely confused
- The activity is clearly too easy
- The activity is clearly too hard
- Players are gaming the rule
- The sport problem has disappeared
- The target behavior is not showing up
- The constraint rewards the wrong thing
- The activity is producing unsafe or unhelpful behavior

## Key Idea

Adjust when the signal is clear.

Do not adjust just because the first version feels imperfect.
