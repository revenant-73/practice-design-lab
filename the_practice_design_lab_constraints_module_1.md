# The Practice Design Lab: Constraints

## Module 1 — What Constraints Actually Do

### Module Goal

By the end of this module, you should understand what a constraint is, what it is not, and why constraints are useful for upgrading activities you already use in practice.

You do not need to memorize theory.

You need to understand one practical idea:

**A constraint is a purposeful change to an activity that shapes what players notice, choose, and do.**

# Screen 2 — What Is a Constraint?

A constraint is a purposeful change to an activity.

That change might involve:

- The space
- The scoring
- The number of players
- The starting situation
- The target
- The time limit
- The pressure
- The rules
- The roles players have
- The behavior of the opponent

When you change one of those things, you change the problem players have to solve.

And when you change the problem, you change what players are invited to notice, choose, and do.

## Key Idea

**A constraint is not just a rule.**

**A constraint is a design choice.**

# Screen 4 — What Constraints Are Not

Constraints can be powerful.

They can also be misused.

A constraint is not:

- A random rule
- A punishment
- A gimmick
- A trick to make practice look creative
- A way to force one perfect answer
- A complicated theory word coaches need to memorize

A good constraint should help players solve a real problem from the sport.

It should not make the activity so strange that players are only trying to satisfy the coach’s rule.

## Quick Check

Which one sounds more like a useful constraint?

**A.** “If you make a mistake, your team runs.”  
**B.** “Your team earns a bonus point when early communication helps a teammate make a better decision.”

**Correct answer:** B

## Feedback

B is better because it shapes attention and behavior inside the activity.

The players are not just avoiding punishment.

They are learning what useful communication looks like, when it matters, and how it helps the team.

# Screen 5 — From Telling to Designing

Coaches often say things like:

“Talk more.”

“Move.”

“Find space.”

“Read the defense.”

“Be more aggressive.”

“Make better decisions.”

Those may all be true.

But if the activity does not create a reason for players to communicate, move, read, support, defend, attack, or choose, the words may not change much.

A constraint helps you move from only telling players what you want to designing a task where the players can feel the problem.

## Example

Instead of only saying:

“Find space.”

You might design:

A small-sided game where a team earns a bonus point when a player receives the ball, pass, or opportunity after creating useful space away from pressure.

Now players have a reason to search for space.

The activity starts teaching the behavior.

# Screen 7 — The Simple Definition Test

A coach should be able to explain constraints without sounding like a textbook.

Here is the working definition for this course:

**A constraint is a purposeful change to an activity that shapes what players notice, choose, and do.**

That definition matters because it gives you three coaching targets:

## Notice

What are players paying attention to?

## Choose

What options are becoming available or unavailable?

## Do

What actions are becoming more likely?

If a constraint does not change what players notice, choose, or do, it may not be doing much.

# Screen 8 — Scenario Check

A basketball coach runs a 3v3 game.

The players keep forcing shots into pressure instead of recognizing open teammates.

The coach wants players to notice the defense and make better decisions.

Which constraint is most useful?

**A.** Players run if they miss two shots in a row.  
**B.** Every player must shoot with their non-dominant hand.  
**C.** A team earns a bonus point when a player passes out of pressure to an open teammate.  
**D.** The coach stops play every time a player makes the wrong choice and explains the correct answer.

**Correct answer:** C

## Feedback

C connects the constraint to the actual problem.

The problem is not effort.

The problem is that players are missing better options under pressure.

The constraint rewards noticing pressure, finding the open teammate, and choosing the better option.

# Screen 11 — Module 1 Summary

Constraints are not magic.

They are not random rules.

They are not about making practice look clever.

A constraint is a purposeful change to an activity that shapes what players notice, choose, and do.

The goal is to make the game problem clearer.

The first step is not adding a rule.

The first step is noticing what the activity is already asking players to do.

## Module Takeaway

**A good constraint upgrades the activity by changing the problem players have to solve.**
