# Screen 2 — Constraints Guide Attention

A constraint is not just a rule.

A constraint is a way to shape what players pay attention to.

If players are missing space, the constraint should help them notice space.

If players are late helping a teammate, the constraint should help them notice the teammate’s problem earlier.

If players rush under pressure, the constraint should help them notice available options before acting.

If players lose focus after mistakes, the constraint should help them notice the next useful action.

The constraint is not the goal.

The goal is better player perception, decision-making, and action.

## Key Idea

Before asking, “What constraint should I add?”

Ask:

> “What do players need to notice?”

# Screen 4 — The Attention Target

An attention target is the specific thing you want players to notice during the activity.

It could be:

- Space
- Teammates
- Opponents
- Timing
- Pressure
- Score
- Support options
- Body position
- Movement cues
- Communication
- Consequences
- Available choices
- The next useful action

This does not mean you tell players exactly what to do every time.

It means you know what the activity is meant to help them notice.

## Example

**Problem:**  
Players crowd the ball when a teammate is under pressure.

**Attention target:**  
Open support space and passing angles.

**Possible constraint later:**  
The team earns a bonus point when a player creates or finds a useful support option away from pressure.

The attention target comes before the constraint.

# Screen 6 — Notice What?

Many coaching problems come from players missing one of four broad information sources.

## 1. Ball / Object

Where is the ball, puck, disc, or object going?

How fast?

How high?

How much time does it create?

## 2. Space

Where is space opening?

Where is space closing?

Where is pressure coming from?

## 3. People

Where are teammates?

Where are opponents?

Who needs help?

Who is available?

## 4. Moment

What is happening right now?

Is this a transition moment?

A pressure moment?

A recovery moment?

A chance to attack?

A chance to reset?

## Key Idea

Players are always noticing something.

The question is whether they are noticing the useful thing soon enough.

# Screen 11 — Attention Target Examples

Use these examples as models.

## Communication

**Problem:**  
Players are not helping teammates early enough.

**Attention target:**  
The teammate’s situation and what information would help before they act.

## Spacing

**Problem:**  
Players crowd the ball.

**Attention target:**  
Open space, support angles, and pressure around the teammate.

## Decision-Making

**Problem:**  
Players force one option.

**Attention target:**  
Opponent position, teammate availability, and risk/reward.

## Defense

**Problem:**  
Players react late.

**Attention target:**  
Opponent movement, body shape, available options, and early cues.

## Transition

**Problem:**  
Players are slow after possession changes.

**Attention target:**  
The moment of change and the next useful space.

## Composure

**Problem:**  
Players carry mistakes into the next action.

**Attention target:**  
The next playable moment, not the previous mistake.

# Screen 16 — Open Response: Make It Observable

A useful attention target should be observable.

Not perfectly measurable.

Observable.

You should be able to watch the activity and ask:

> Are players noticing this earlier, more clearly, or more often?

## Less Observable

Players need to be smarter.

Players need more confidence.

Players need to care more.

Players need to understand the game.

## More Observable

Players notice open support space before the teammate gets trapped.

Players recognize opponent pressure before choosing.

Players communicate useful information before a teammate acts.

Players recover into useful space immediately after losing possession.

# Screen 18 — The Attention Target Check

Before moving on, check your attention target.

Can you answer yes to these?

1. Does it connect to the clear practice problem?
2. Does it name specific information players need to notice?
3. Does it include when that information matters?
4. Is it observable during the activity?
5. Could it happen inside a real game?
6. Could a constraint help bring it into focus?
7. Could another coach understand what you mean?

If you have at least five yes answers, you are ready to move on.

If not, revise your attention target.

## Revision Prompt

Make your attention target clearer by adding:

- What players should notice
- When they should notice it
- Why it matters
