# The Practice Design Lab: Constraints

## Module 2 — Start With the Problem

### Module Goal

By the end of this module, you should be able to turn a vague coaching concern into a clear practice problem.

This matters because a constraint only works when it is connected to the problem you actually want players to solve.

If the problem is vague, the constraint will probably be vague too.

If the problem is clear, the constraint becomes much easier to design.

# Screen 2 — Why Vague Problems Create Weak Constraints

Most coaches can quickly name what they want to improve.

Communication.

Decision-making.

Spacing.

Aggression.

Defense.

Passing.

Transition.

Composure.

But those are not clear practice problems yet.

They are categories.

A category tells you the general area.

A clear problem tells you what is actually happening, when it happens, and what players may need to notice or solve differently.

## Example

**Vague:**  
“We need better communication.”

That may be true.

But what does it mean?

Do players need to speak earlier?

Say more useful information?

Claim responsibility?

Give help to a teammate?

Coordinate movement?

Reset after mistakes?

Those are different problems.

They probably need different constraints.

## Key Idea

A vague problem points in a direction.

A clear problem gives you something to design around.

# Screen 4 — The Problem Formula

Use this simple formula:

## Players are...

What are players doing or not doing?

## When...

In what situation does it happen?

## Which leads to...

What problem does it create?

## They may need to...

What might players need to notice, coordinate, choose, or do differently?

## Example

**Vague:**  
“We need better spacing.”

**Clearer:**  
Players are crowding the ball when a teammate is under pressure, which removes passing options and makes the next action easier for the opponent to defend. They may need to notice open space and support angles earlier.

This is not perfect.

It does not need to be perfect.

It is clear enough to design from.

# Screen 7 — The Three-Part Diagnosis Check

A useful practice problem usually includes three things:

## 1. The Situation

When does the problem happen?

Examples:

- In transition
- After a mistake
- When under pressure
- When receiving from a teammate
- When defending space
- When the opponent changes shape
- When the score gets close

## 2. The Player Behavior

What are players doing or not doing?

Examples:

- Moving late
- Crowding the ball
- Staying quiet
- Forcing one option
- Waiting too long
- Losing spacing
- Watching instead of supporting
- Rushing the next action

## 3. The Consequence

Why does it matter?

Examples:

- Fewer options
- Easier for opponent to defend
- Poorer support
- Lost scoring chance
- Slower transition
- Less useful communication
- More panic decisions
- Less pressure on the opponent

## Key Idea

If your problem includes the situation, behavior, and consequence, you are much closer to a useful constraint.

# Screen 9 — Scenario: Choose the Better Problem

A soccer coach says:

> “We need to keep possession better.”

Which version is more useful for designing a constraint?

A. “Players need better possession.”  
B. “Players are panicking when pressured near the sideline.”  
C. “Players are forcing passes down the line near the sideline, which traps the next teammate and makes possession easier to lose.”  
D. “Players need to be more confident.”

**Correct answer:** C

## Feedback

C is the strongest because it includes the situation, behavior, and consequence.

- Situation: near the sideline
- Behavior: forcing passes down the line
- Consequence: trapping the next teammate and losing possession

That gives the coach something specific to design around.

# Screen 10 — Scenario: What Problem Is Hiding Under the Complaint?

A basketball coach keeps saying:

> “We need to stop standing around.”

Which clearer problem might be hiding underneath that complaint?

Choose the best answer.

A. “The players are lazy.”  
B. “Players away from the ball are not creating passing lanes when the ball handler is pressured.”  
C. “They need to run more.”  
D. “They do not care enough.”

**Correct answer:** B

## Feedback

B turns the complaint into a design problem.

It avoids judging the players and identifies what needs to change inside the activity.

The issue may not be effort.

The issue may be that players do not recognize when, where, or how to support the teammate with the ball.

# Screen 11 — Reflection: Remove the Judgment

Coaches often describe problems with judgment words.

Lazy.

Quiet.

Soft.

Careless.

Unfocused.

Passive.

Selfish.

Those words may describe how it feels to the coach.

But they usually do not help design better practice.

Try translating judgment into observable behavior.

## Instead of:

“They are lazy.”

Try:

“Players are slow to recover after losing the ball.”

## Instead of:

“They are selfish.”

Try:

“Players are forcing low-percentage options instead of recognizing open teammates.”

## Instead of:

“They are quiet.”

Try:

“Players are not giving useful information early enough to help a teammate act.”

# Screen 13 — Common Problem Categories

If you feel stuck, start with one of these common team-sport problem categories.

Then make it more specific.

## Space

Players may not be recognizing open space, creating space, protecting space, or using space well.

## Timing

Players may be acting too early, too late, or without reading the moment.

## Support

Players may not be helping the teammate with the ball, puck, or immediate problem.

## Pressure

Players may be rushing, hiding, forcing, or freezing when the challenge increases.

## Communication

Players may be saying too little, saying too much, saying it too late, or giving information that does not help.

## Decision-Making

Players may be choosing before looking, forcing one option, or missing better options.

## Transition

Players may be slow to switch roles after gaining or losing possession, control, or advantage.

## Composure

Players may be carrying the last mistake into the next action.

## Key Idea

The category is only the starting point.

Your job is to make it specific enough to design around.

# Screen 15 — Multi-Sport Examples

Use these examples as models.

## Volleyball

**Vague:**  
“We need better serve receive.”

**Clearer:**  
Passers are late recognizing short/deep serve depth, which creates rushed first contacts and fewer attacking options.

## Basketball

**Vague:**  
“We need better spacing.”

**Clearer:**  
Off-ball players drift toward the ball when the ball handler is pressured, which removes passing lanes and makes help defense easier.

## Soccer

**Vague:**  
“We need to defend better.”

**Clearer:**  
After losing possession, players are slow to recover central space, which gives the opponent an easier path forward.

## Hockey

**Vague:**  
“We need better puck movement.”

**Clearer:**  
Players hold the puck too long under pressure instead of recognizing the early support option.

## Lacrosse

**Vague:**  
“We need to attack smarter.”

**Clearer:**  
Players drive into help defense without reading whether the adjacent defender has committed.

## Key Idea

The clearer version does not need to solve the problem yet.

It just needs to describe the problem well enough that a constraint can be designed.

# Screen 18 — Self-Check: Is Your Problem Clear Enough?

Before moving on, check your problem statement.

Can you answer yes to these?

1. Does it describe what players are doing or not doing?
2. Does it name when the problem happens?
3. Does it explain why the problem matters?
4. Does it avoid judging the players as people?
5. Could another coach understand what you mean?
6. Could this problem happen inside a real game?
7. Does it give you something to design around?

If you have at least five yes answers, you are ready to move on.

If not, revise the statement.

## Revision Prompt

Make the problem more specific by adding:

- When it happens
- What players are doing
- Why it matters
