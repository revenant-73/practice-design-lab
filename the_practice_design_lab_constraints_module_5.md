# Screen 2 — Lever vs. Constraint

Choosing a lever is not the same as creating the constraint.

## Lever

Scoring

## Constraint

The team earns a bonus point when early, useful communication helps a teammate make a better decision.

---

## Lever

Space

## Constraint

The team can only score if the final action comes from a player who created or found useful space away from pressure.

---

## Lever

Starting Situation

## Constraint

Each round begins immediately after a turnover, and the team has three seconds to recover into useful defensive positions.

---

The lever points you in the right direction.

The constraint tells the players what actually changes.

## Key Idea

A good constraint is specific enough to run, but simple enough to explain quickly.

# Screen 5 — The Anatomy of an Upgraded Activity

A useful activity upgrade includes six parts.

## 1. Original Activity

What are you already running?

## 2. Problem

What is breaking down?

## 3. Attention Target

What do players need to notice?

## 4. Constraint

What one thing will you change?

## 5. Success Condition

How will players know what matters?

## 6. Coaching Question

What question will help players reflect without giving them every answer?

## Key Idea

If you can fill in these six parts, you have a usable first version.

It does not need to be perfect.

It needs to be clear enough to test.

# Screen 7 — What Makes a Constraint Meaningful?

A meaningful constraint should do at least one of these things:

- Make useful information easier to notice
- Make a better decision more valuable
- Make an unhelpful habit less useful
- Create a reason to communicate
- Create a reason to support
- Increase responsibility
- Preserve game-like pressure
- Make the consequence of a choice clearer
- Keep the activity connected to the sport

A weak constraint usually does one of these things:

- Adds complexity without purpose
- Rewards something unrelated
- Punishes mistakes without teaching
- Makes the activity feel disconnected
- Forces one answer too tightly
- Solves a different problem than the one you identified

## Key Idea

A constraint is meaningful when it changes the problem in the direction of the learning goal.

# Screen 9 — Worked Example: Space Constraint

## Original Activity

Small-sided possession game.

## Problem

Players crowd the ball when a teammate is under pressure.

## Attention Target

Open support space and passing angles.

## Lever

Space.

## Constraint

A team earns a bonus point when a player creates or finds useful support space before the team completes the next action.

## Success Condition

The support player must be available away from pressure, not just standing nearby.

## Coaching Question

“Where could you become useful before your teammate ran out of options?”

## Why It Works

The constraint makes space matter.

Players are not just told to spread out.

They are rewarded for recognizing where support is needed and moving into a useful position.

---

# Screen 10 — Worked Example: Scoring Constraint

## Original Activity

Small-sided competitive game.

## Problem

Players make safe choices that do not challenge the opponent.

## Attention Target

Moments when pressure can be created.

## Lever

Scoring.

## Constraint

A team scores two points when their action clearly creates pressure on the opponent, and one point for a normal score.

## Success Condition

The action must make the opponent rushed, stretched, disorganized, or forced into a lower-quality response.

## Coaching Question

“What choice made the opponent’s problem harder?”

## Why It Works

The constraint changes what the activity rewards.

Players are not just playing to keep the ball safe.

They are learning to recognize when and how they can create pressure.

---

# Screen 11 — Worked Example: Number Constraint

## Original Activity

Full-team scrimmage or large-sided game.

## Problem

Some players are hiding from decisions.

## Attention Target

When and where to support the current action.

## Lever

Number of players.

## Constraint

Reduce the activity to a smaller-sided version where each player has more responsibility and fewer places to hide.

## Success Condition

Every player must be involved as either an option, support player, defender, communicator, or recovery player during each sequence.

## Coaching Question

“How were you connected to the play when you did not have the ball?”

## Why It Works

The constraint changes involvement.

Smaller numbers usually increase touches, decisions, communication, and responsibility.

---

# Screen 12 — Worked Example: Starting Situation Constraint

## Original Activity

Normal possession or transition game.

## Problem

Players are slow to recover after losing possession.

## Attention Target

The moment possession changes and the next useful recovery space.

## Lever

Starting situation.

## Constraint

Every round starts immediately after a turnover, loose ball, or change of possession.

## Success Condition

The team must recover into useful defensive or support positions within three seconds.

## Coaching Question

“What changed the moment possession changed?”

## Why It Works

The constraint places players directly into the moment they need to solve.

Instead of waiting for the problem to appear randomly, the activity begins there.

---

# Screen 13 — Worked Example: Rule Constraint

## Original Activity

Small-sided attacking game.

## Problem

Players force the same option even when the opponent takes it away.

## Attention Target

Opponent position and alternative options.

## Lever

Rules.

## Constraint

The team cannot score using the same final option twice in a row.

## Success Condition

Players must recognize what the opponent is protecting and search for a different useful option.

## Coaching Question

“What did the opponent take away, and what did that open?”

## Why It Works

The constraint nudges players away from automatic repetition.

It does not tell them exactly what to do.

It asks them to read the situation and search for another solution.

---

# Screen 14 — Worked Example: Role Constraint

## Original Activity

Small-sided team game.

## Problem

Players communicate late or give information that does not help.

## Attention Target

A teammate’s situation and what information would help before they act.

## Lever

Roles.

## Constraint

One player is assigned as the information player. The team earns a bonus point when that player gives early, useful information that helps a teammate make a better decision.

## Success Condition

The information must be early enough and specific enough to help the teammate act.

## Coaching Question

“What information actually helped your teammate?”

## Why It Works

The constraint gives communication a purpose.

It moves players away from noise and toward useful information.

---

# Screen 15 — Worked Example: Pressure Constraint

## Original Activity

Regular small-sided or full-team game.

## Problem

Players make good decisions in easy moments but rush when the score is close.

## Attention Target

Available options under pressure.

## Lever

Pressure.

## Constraint

Each round starts in a close-score situation, and the team must win two pressure points before rotating.

## Success Condition

Players must continue recognizing useful options while the outcome matters.

## Coaching Question

“What option did you see under pressure?”

## Why It Works

The constraint keeps the problem connected to the pressure moment.

It does not remove the challenge.

It helps players learn inside it.

---

# Screen 16 — Worked Example: Opponent Behavior Constraint

## Original Activity

Small-sided attacking game.

## Problem

Players do not recognize when the opponent leaves space open.

## Attention Target

Opponent position and available space.

## Lever

Opponent behavior.

## Constraint

The defending team starts by overloading one side, creating open space somewhere else.

## Success Condition

The attacking team must identify and use the available space before the defense can recover.

## Coaching Question

“What did the opponent give you?”

## Why It Works

The constraint makes the opponent’s behavior easier to read at first.

Over time, the opponent can become less predictable.

# Screen 21 — Make the Success Condition Clear

A constraint needs a success condition.

The success condition tells players what counts.

Without it, the activity can become confusing.

## Weak

“Communicate better.”

## Stronger

“The team earns a bonus point when communication is early, specific, and helps a teammate make a better decision.”

## Weak

“Use space.”

## Stronger

“The team earns a bonus point when a player creates or finds useful space away from pressure before the team scores.”

## Weak

“Recover faster.”

## Stronger

“After losing possession, the team has three seconds to recover into useful defensive positions.”

## Key Idea

The success condition should make the constraint visible.

# Screen 27 — Self-Check: Is Your Upgrade Ready to Test?

Before moving on, check your activity upgrade.

Can you answer yes to these?

1. Does the constraint connect to the clear practice problem?
2. Does it connect to the attention target?
3. Is it based on one main lever?
4. Can you explain it quickly?
5. Does the activity still feel connected to the sport?
6. Does the success condition make sense?
7. Does the coaching question point attention back to the problem?
8. Could you run this tomorrow?

If you have at least six yes answers, you have a usable first version.

If not, simplify.

## Revision Prompt

If your upgrade feels too complicated, remove one rule, one bonus point, or one extra condition.

Then ask:

> What is the one change that matters most?

# Screen 28 — Common Build Mistakes

## Mistake 1: The Constraint Does Not Match the Problem

If the problem is spacing, but the constraint rewards effort, the match is weak.

## Mistake 2: The Constraint Has Too Many Rules

If players need a long explanation, simplify.

## Mistake 3: The Success Condition Is Unclear

If no one knows what counts, the constraint will not guide attention.

## Mistake 4: The Activity Stops Feeling Like the Sport

If the game problem disappears, the constraint may be too artificial.

## Mistake 5: The Coach Gives Away the Answer Too Quickly

Let players search.

Use questions to guide attention without solving every moment for them.
